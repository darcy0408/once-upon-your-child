Every story in Once Upon YOUR Child is built to do two jobs at once: delight your child, and
quietly practice a real emotional skill. This page explains — in checkable terms, mapped to the
frameworks schools actually use — what those skills are and how the stories teach them.

## The short version

- Our content is designed around **CASEL's five social-emotional competencies** — the framework
  used by school districts across the US — and cross-referenced against the **American School
  Counselor Association's (ASCA) Mindsets & Behaviors** standards.
- Stories teach through a well-studied mechanism: a child identifying with a character who faces
  a relatable emotional situation, at a safe distance from real-world stakes.
- We are honest about limits. This app **supports** social-emotional learning; it is **not
  therapy**, it does not diagnose or treat anything, and it does not replace a counselor,
  therapist, or — most importantly — you.

## The five skills, and how stories build them

**1. Self-awareness — naming what you feel.**
Before a child can manage a big feeling, they need a word for it. Our youngest readers (ages 3–5)
meet feelings as friendly animal companions — a sunny pup, a rainy bunny, a roaring lion, a shy
mouse — because at that age, feelings need faces before they need vocabulary. Older kids work
with a richer emotion set: not just happy and sad, but *frustrated*, *embarrassed*, *worried* —
each paired with how it actually feels in your body ("ready to explode"). Across ages, stories
teach kids to notice the early body signal — we call it the "uh-oh feeling" — before it becomes a
reaction. This is the skill our content covers most deeply.

**2. Self-management — what to do with the feeling.**
Stories pause at the tense moment and practice a concrete, physical calming skill with the child —
paced breathing like "Dragon's Breath," or grounding in what you can see and touch — the same
techniques SEL programs use in classrooms. The child practices alongside a story companion, right
when the story (and the reader) feels the tension. These techniques are real and evidence-informed;
we're honest that our deepest coverage here is calming skills, not goal-setting or study habits.

**3. Social awareness — reading how someone else feels.**
Quests like *Standing Tall* ask the reader to notice a classmate who's been shrunk by unkindness
and choose how to respond. Boundary stories teach the other side of the "uh-oh feeling" too:
watching *someone else's* face for it, and stopping. One honest gap we're working on: our current
stories don't yet specifically practice perspective-taking across cultural difference.

**4. Relationship skills — saying the hard thing.**
Kids rehearse real scripts in a safe fictional frame: asking to join a group, saying "no thank
you" and seeing that a real friend stays, standing up for a friend, asking a trusted grown-up
for help. Then — and this is deliberate — many quests end with a **tip for the grown-up**, a
conversation starter that hands the skill from the story to your kitchen table. The story is the
rehearsal; you're the real thing.

**5. Responsible decision-making — choosing, and living with the choice.**
Our choose-your-path quests let a child make a choice and watch the consequence unfold — safely,
in fiction, where a wrong turn costs nothing and teaches everything. The choices are
deliberately authored (2–4 thoughtful options, not an open world): that's a safety decision, and
it means we teach *guided* decision-making, with reflection prompts for teens after each choice.

## For educators and school counselors

We mapped our content against all 36 of ASCA's Mindsets & Behaviors standards — using ASCA's own
published crosswalk to the CASEL 5 — and we publish the honest tally rather than a highlight reel:
our content **directly and strongly addresses 4 standards** (whole-self wellbeing, effective
coping skills, managing transitions and change, and self/peer advocacy — that last one is the
entire thesis of our boundary-setting stories), **partially addresses 14 more**, and **does not
target 18** — most of which are academic and career-readiness standards that a storytelling app
shouldn't pretend to teach. The full standard-by-standard table is available on request.

## Built for the age your child actually is

The same skill looks different at 4 than at 14, so the same quest is a different story in each
age band — matched to CASEL's own developmental benchmarks:

| Ages | What SEL looks like at this age | How stories match it |
|---|---|---|
| **3–5** | Feelings need names and faces first | Animal feeling-friends, body cues ("uh-oh feeling in your tummy"), simple and concrete |
| **6–8** | Can follow concrete steps, not abstract advice | Timed, literal calming skills practiced in-story; first "no thank you" scripts |
| **9–12** | Can predict consequences and weigh options | Branching choices with visible outcomes; richer emotion vocabulary |
| **13–17** | Identity questions; abstract self-reflection | The same dilemmas become realistic and internal — "who are you when no one's watching?" — with reflection prompts after each choice |

## The evidence behind the approach

The teaching approach rests on two well-established bodies of research (these are findings about
SEL and story-based learning generally — **not clinical trials of this app**, and we won't
pretend otherwise):

- **Structured SEL works.** The landmark meta-analysis of school-based SEL programs (Durlak et
  al., 2011, *Child Development* — 213 programs, 270,000+ students) found significant gains in
  social-emotional skills, behavior, and even academics; a 2023 update at nearly twice the scale
  (Cipriano et al., *Child Development* — 424 studies, 53 countries) confirmed the findings hold.
- **Stories are a real delivery vehicle.** Research on bibliotherapy — using stories to support
  emotional wellbeing — shows meaningful benefits for children and adolescents (Yuan et al., 2018,
  meta-analysis of 8 randomized trials), working through exactly the mechanism our app is built
  on: identifying with a character facing a relatable situation, at a safe narrative distance.

## What we don't claim

We think honesty is a feature. So, plainly:

- **This is not therapy** and does not replace a licensed professional. If your child is
  struggling, please talk to one — the app also surfaces free crisis resources (988, Crisis Text
  Line) wherever stories touch heavier topics.
- **CASEL and ASCA have not reviewed or endorsed this product.** They are school frameworks; our
  mapping shows design alignment, not certification.
- **This specific app has not been clinically trialed.** The research above supports the
  approach; personalized AI-generated stories are a newer format than those studies tested.
- **Every story is safety-screened**, and heavier themes come with a parent heads-up before the
  story starts — you'll always know before your child reads.
