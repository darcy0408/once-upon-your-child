# Content Samples for Clinical Review — Once Upon YOUR Child

**What this is:** Item 3 of the five-part review packet described in the engagement playbook
("What to send them once they say yes"). Items 1 and 2 — the CASEL/ASCA framework mapping and the
adolescent "antihero" safety review packet — are separate documents already prepared; this
document does not repeat them. This document was assembled 2026-07-05.

**How to read this:** You do not need any technical background. Below are real samples of what a
child or teen actually sees and reads inside the app — story text, the choices they're offered, and
the short notes written for parents — presented as plain reading. Two of the three Life Quest
samples are AI-generated from a fixed set of instructions rather than word-for-word scripted; where
that's the case, this document shows you the instructions themselves (what the story is required to
do) rather than one arbitrary generated output.

## 1. Life Quest samples

"Life Quest" is the app's name for a short, choice-driven story built around a single everyday
social-emotional challenge (being left out, a sibling fight, a friend's mean joke, a big feeling
that won't settle). A child picks a path through 2–4 short choices and reaches one of several
possible endings. Three samples below span the age range and the three types of content requested
for this review: an early-childhood emotional-regulation story, a story that runs across every age
band with different framing at each stage, and the boundary-setting skill line.

### 1.1 "The Big NO" — ages 3–5 (Sprout band), Big Feelings section

**Skill it teaches:** naming and riding out a big burst of anger/frustration without it controlling
the child, and — as the closing line reinforces — that a strong feeling passing doesn't mean the
child "loses."

**Companion guide:** Roary Lion, one of four animal guides in the Sprout "Big Feelings" section
(the others: Sunny Pup for happy, Rainy Bunny for sad, Shy Mouse for scared). Each guide anchors one
feeling family so a 3–5-year-old has a concrete character to associate with an emotion rather than
an abstract word.

**The story, as a child experiences it:**

> You see the cookies on the counter. "Cookie, please?" The grown-up shakes their head. "Not
> before lunch, love." NOOOO! Your hands go tight. Your face goes HOT. A big mad lion roars inside
> you.

At this point the child is offered a short, guided breathing exercise ("Dragon's Breath") before
choosing how the lion-mad comes out: **"Stomp your feet"** or **"Roar it out like a dragon."**

- If the child chooses to stomp: the grown-up sits down on the rug and says, *"I see your big mad.
  Mad is okay. Stomping is okay too — as long as nobody gets hurt."* The child can keep stomping
  until the mad gets smaller, or ask when they can have a cookie.
- If the child chooses to roar: the story has the child take a big lion-breath and roar it out,
  and the grown-up roars back, "a little quieter, a little funnier" — they both end up laughing.
  The text notes plainly: *"Mad is still here. But it is smaller now."*

Both paths converge on the same two closing choices — keep stomping until calm, ask *"when CAN I
have a cookie?"*, or pick a different snack while waiting. Every ending affirms the feeling rather
than punishing it. Representative endings:

> "That was a big mad," they say. "You let it out. You did good, [child's name]." Mad lions get
> tired too.

> "When CAN I have a cookie?" "After lunch. So in a little bit." "Pinky promise?" "Pinky promise."
> ... Waiting is easier when you know when. And mad turned into a question.

> You pick the cheese. Crunch, crunch. It is not a cookie. But it is yummy too. And after lunch —
> there is still a cookie waiting. You did not lose. You waited.

**The grown-up-facing note shown at the end (not shown to the child as part of the story):**
*"Ask: 'When you hear NO, what does your body do? What can you ask instead?'"* — a one-line prompt
inviting the parent to turn the story into a real conversation.

### 1.2 "Standing Tall" — a story that runs across every age band (3 to 18+)

Unlike "The Big NO," this one is not scripted word-for-word — the app generates the actual prose
each time from a fixed, detailed set of story instructions (a "world bible"), so the plot beats,
imagery, and vocabulary are locked but the exact sentences a child reads vary. This lets the review
see the therapeutic design intent directly. All versions dramatize the same underlying situation —
someone using unkindness to make another person feel small — but the concreteness of the metaphor
and the resolution scale up with developmental stage.

**Skill it teaches:** noticing and responding to social aggression (bullying/exclusion) without
retaliation, in a way built around composure, seeking help, and solidarity rather than victory over
an opponent.

**Ages 3–5 and 6–8 (concrete, embodied metaphor):** The story world is a playground where a
"grumpy shadow" is making other kids feel small — literally shrinking them in the fiction each time
someone is unkind. The instructions require the story to give the child protagonist a warm, glowing
feeling in their chest that grows brighter with each act of courage, and to have the shadow shrink
only when confronted "calmly and firmly," never through force. A quiet friend who has already been
"shrunk" needs encouragement; a teacher notices but waits to see if the child will try first. The
instructed takeaway line, given directly to younger readers: *"Being brave means using your words
and asking a grown-up for help,"* and for the story world overall: *"standing up doesn't mean
standing alone."*

**Ages 9–12 and teens (realistic framing, "no magical solutions"):** The same situation is
reframed as an ordinary, realistic school setting — public embarrassment, exclusion, rumor-spreading
— and the instructions explicitly forbid a magical fix. The story is required to dramatize the
internal pull between wanting to retaliate and the strength it takes to stay composed, the fear of
looking weak against the courage of walking away, and the temptation to become as unkind as the
person causing harm. The instructed strategies are all real-world ones: documenting what happened,
building alliances, setting firm limits, knowing when to bring in an adult. The instructed "victory"
is explicitly not that the other person changes — it's that the protagonist keeps their own
self-respect.

**Ages 13–14 (identity framing):** The same scenario is pitched as an identity question rather than
a plot to resolve, introduced with the on-screen prompt: *"Who are you when no one's watching?"*

**Adults (18+):** The instructed framing shifts again, to *"holding your ground without losing
yourself — or becoming what you are standing against."*

### 1.3 Boundary-setting skill line — "It's Okay to Say No" / "When Someone Says Stop"

This is the app's dedicated boundary-setting content, added as a selectable story focus a parent can
turn on. Like "Standing Tall," the actual story prose is generated per-story from a fixed
instruction set rather than scripted once; what's fixed and shown below are the skill definitions,
the exact coping-language lines the instructions require the story to use, and the situations the
generator is told to draw from.

**"It's Okay to Say No"** — teaches a child to set a limit and trust their own internal warning
signal. The instructions require the generated story to have the character:

- Notice the "uh-oh" feeling in their tummy
- Say "stop" or "no thank you"
- Walk away and tell a trusted grown-up
- Land on the idea that a real friend stays even after you say no

The situations the generator is told to draw one from: a friend who keeps asking for something
that doesn't feel fair; someone wanting a hug the character doesn't want to give; a "secret" that
makes the character's tummy feel funny. The required positive outcome: the character sets the
limit calmly and still feels safe and liked afterward.

**"When Someone Says Stop"** — the companion skill, teaching a child to respect *other* people's
limits immediately. Required story beats:

- Stop the first time someone says stop
- Ask before touching, borrowing, or hugging
- Watch faces and feelings for an "uh-oh"
- Give a friend space when they need it

Drawn-from situations: a friend who doesn't want to play the same game anymore; someone needing
quiet time alone; a companion character saying "please don't" during a game. Required positive
outcome: the character becomes the kind of friend other people trust.

The phrase **"uh-oh feeling"** is used deliberately and consistently as the app's plain-language
stand-in for interoceptive awareness (noticing a bodily/emotional warning signal before acting on
it) across both the youngest boundary content and the general emotional-vocabulary content for
older bands.

## 2. Adolescent "antihero" story samples (ages 15–17)

**Status: representative full story texts are not currently available to include here.** The
antihero safety review (item 2 of this packet) is backed by an evidence batch of seven full story
generations, hand-read and summarized in that document — but the raw generated story text from that
batch run was written to a temporary working folder that has since been cleared, and was never
checked into the product's permanent files. Nothing has been fabricated to fill the gap. Two things
are available in the meantime:

**(a) Verified excerpts already on record.** These two fragments are quoted directly in the safety
review packet's findings and can be shared as representative flavor, with the caveat that they are
short excerpts, not full stories:

> *Worst-case single story (distress disclosed, teen's coping style is to withdraw, no one named
> to confide in):* "[The friend] didn't leap to fix… 'We'll figure it out. Not for show. With
> people.'" The story's aftermath: "a friend knew she wasn't fine and had not walked away… the
> first light after rain."

> *Worst-case interactive story (the teen reader deliberately chooses to keep hiding rather than
> open up):* the story routes a moment of being cared for through a different character instead —
> "You can stop carrying that alone… She doesn't try to fix me" — and frames the continued hiding
> as a cost (a strained friendship), never as a win. It ends on an opening: a soft knock at the
> door.

**(b) An exact recipe to regenerate a fresh batch before the review call**, if 2–3 full stories are
wanted for the clinician to read directly (recommended — this takes a few minutes and costs a small
amount against the developer's own API budget, no cost to the reviewer):

Run the project's prepared evidence-batch script from the root of the codebase. It calls the same
production AI model the app itself uses, feeds it the app's real story instructions, and tests the
single hardest case the 15–17 band allows: a teen who discloses "That I'm not okay" in several
combinations with how they cope and who (if anyone) they let in. It automatically flags anything
resembling substances, self-harm framing, violence, or sexual content for a human to double-check,
and separately checks whether the story reaches the required "someone notices and responds with
care" moment. All seven raw story texts, plus a scored summary table, are saved to a folder for
reading — the location is printed at the end of the run.

Before sending 2–3 of those regenerated stories to the reviewer, a quick human skim for the one
known soft spot is worth doing: about 1 in 7 generations lets an incidental background detail
(overheard party noise, a passing mention of drinking) slip in as scenery, even though it is not
used or endorsed by any character. It has never appeared as the point of a scene, only as
atmosphere — but it is worth confirming it isn't present in whichever 2–3 stories get sent.

## 3. What this app is not

Once Upon YOUR Child is a personalized storytelling app that supports the development of
social-emotional skills — it is not therapy, not a diagnostic tool, and not a treatment for any
mental-health condition. Everywhere the app or its marketing describes what it does, the intended
language is that it "supports" social-emotional learning, never that it "treats" or "resolves" a
child's emotional or behavioral difficulty. It is designed to complement, never substitute for,
the care and judgment of a parent, caregiver, teacher, or licensed mental-health professional —
and for any child who appears to be in real distress, the product routes toward a trusted adult
and (for teens) toward real crisis resources, rather than attempting to resolve that distress
inside the fiction itself.
