# Big Feelings Theme: Older-Kid Product Spec

## Scope

This spec defines the older-child version of `Big Feelings` as one of the five story themes inside the existing story product, not a separate mode.

Primary target:
- Ages 9-12

Secondary direction:
- Teens 13-15 where relevant

Core outcome:
- Help children name what they feel with more precision
- Show calming as regaining choice, not shutting emotion down
- Normalize mistakes and support repair after harm
- Teach through story consequences and character decisions, not explicit lessons

## Product Positioning

`Big Feelings` for older kids should feel like:
- A socially real story about pressure, conflict, and recovery
- Emotionally intelligent, not therapeutic-sounding
- Supportive without sounding soft or babyish
- Reflective, but still active and plot-driven

It should not feel like:
- A coping worksheet
- A lesson disguised as a story
- A cute feelings game
- A punishment arc where the child is corrected by adults

## Narrative Design Principles

1. The emotion is not the problem. The pressure, misunderstanding, impulse, or fallout is the problem.
2. The story should validate intensity without rewarding harm.
3. Calming should create a better next move, not erase the feeling.
4. Repair should feel brave and credible, not tidy or instant.
5. Choices should change social outcomes in believable ways.
6. Adults can steady the scene, but the child protagonist must retain agency.

## Theme Entry In Product

Theme label for ages 9-12:
- `Big Feelings`

Optional subtitle in UI:
- `Stories about pressure, conflict, and what you do next`

Teen-facing label if age 13-15 uses variant copy:
- `Big Feelings`

Teen subtitle:
- `Stories about tension, identity, and repair`

Do not use:
- `Taming big feelings`
- `Calm down stories`
- `Let's talk about feelings`

## Age 9-12 Story Flow

Recommended theme flow:

1. Feeling signal
- The opening shows the protagonist already under pressure.
- The feeling is shown through body cues, attention shifts, or social interpretation before it is named directly.

2. Trigger event
- Something specific happens: exclusion, embarrassment, accusation, unfairness, public pressure, sudden change, broken trust.

3. First move
- The protagonist makes an instinctive choice.
- This choice may be messy, defensive, avoidant, sharp, or impulsive.

4. Consequence
- The world reacts.
- A friend pulls back, a rumor spreads, a team loses trust, the protagonist feels worse, or a chance closes.

5. Regulation beat
- The protagonist gets a believable pause.
- This is not a lecture scene. It is a moment to reset enough to choose on purpose.

6. Second choice
- The protagonist chooses a response path: boundary, truth, apology, problem-solving, asking for space, asking for help, or repair attempt.

7. Social response
- The other character does not always respond perfectly.
- The story should show partial progress as a valid win.

8. Resolution
- The ending restores agency and clarity, not perfect harmony.
- The protagonist leaves with a stronger read on self, relationship, or next step.

## Teen / High-Level Direction

For ages 13-15, keep the same structure but shift the emotional frame:
- More internal contradiction
- More identity and reputation stakes
- More ambiguity in friendships
- Less overt coaching language
- More room for unresolved but grounded endings

Teen stories can hold:
- mixed motives
- social risk
- disappointment after doing the right thing
- bittersweet repair

Teen stories should still avoid:
- adult therapist voice
- moral summary paragraphs
- melodrama that frames every conflict as life-defining

## Nuanced Feeling List

Use a layered vocabulary model for ages 9-12. Surface nuanced words early instead of forcing only basic emotions.

### Core feeling families

Anger / activation:
- irritated
- annoyed
- frustrated
- resentful
- wronged
- furious
- heated
- defensive

Anxiety / fear:
- worried
- uneasy
- tense
- overwhelmed
- on edge
- apprehensive
- panicked
- exposed

Sadness / hurt:
- disappointed
- left out
- hurt
- discouraged
- heavy
- ashamed
- heartsick
- numb

Embarrassment / self-conscious emotion:
- awkward
- embarrassed
- self-conscious
- stupid
- humiliated
- foolish
- regretful
- exposed

Loneliness / disconnection:
- alone
- ignored
- disconnected
- unwanted
- unseen
- homesick
- isolated
- replaced

Confusion / mixed state:
- conflicted
- thrown off
- uncertain
- suspicious
- stuck
- torn
- overstimulated
- scrambled

Positive-but-complex states:
- relieved
- proud
- included
- hopeful
- steady
- understood
- brave
- lighter

Rules for usage:
- The story can start with body-state language before naming the feeling.
- Prefer one primary feeling and one secondary feeling.
- Mixed feelings are appropriate and should appear often.
- Avoid overloading the story with more than three named feeling words.

## Social Conflict Categories

The older-kid `Big Feelings` theme should pull from socially real conflict buckets:

1. Friendship strain
- being left out
- shifting alliances
- canceled plans
- private chat exclusion
- feeling replaced

2. Public embarrassment
- wrong answer in class
- tripping, dropping, or failing in front of peers
- rumor or screenshot-style exposure
- being laughed at

3. Fairness conflict
- sibling gets different rules
- teammate gets credit
- adult misunderstands what happened
- consequences feel unequal

4. Boundary conflict
- unwanted teasing
- pressure to share secrets
- friend copying work
- someone keeps pushing after "stop"

5. Mistake and fallout
- saying something mean
- rage-texting or blurting
- breaking trust
- lying to avoid trouble

6. Performance pressure
- sports tryout
- audition
- presentation
- test stress
- fear of letting people down

7. Belonging and identity
- not fitting one group
- code-switching socially
- liking different things than peers
- feeling "too much" or "not enough"

## Emotional Skill Patterns Inside Story

The story should teach skill through action patterns, not named SEL lessons.

### Self-talk patterns

Allowed internal lines:
- `I'm getting flooded. I need ten seconds.`
- `Being mad doesn't mean I'm right about everything.`
- `I can be upset and still choose what I do next.`
- `If I say this now, I might make it worse.`
- `I don't have to pretend I'm fine.`
- `I can tell the truth without blowing this up.`

What self-talk should do:
- notice escalation
- separate feeling from action
- reduce shame spirals
- support one concrete next move

Avoid:
- affirmation-card language
- `I am perfect`
- `just calm down`
- long inner monologues that sound like therapy scripts

### Boundary patterns

Useful forms:
- `Don't joke about that with me.`
- `I'm not talking while people are watching.`
- `You can be mad, but don't grab my stuff.`
- `I need space. I'll come back.`
- `That story about me isn't true. Stop repeating it.`

Boundary writing rules:
- short
- clear
- emotionally credible
- no fake serenity

### Repair patterns

Repair can include:
- admitting impact without a speech
- correcting the story
- replacing damaged trust with an action
- giving someone space after apologizing
- asking what would help now

Repair should not require:
- instant forgiveness
- full friendship restoration
- the protagonist being purely innocent

### Apology patterns

Preferred apology forms:
- `I snapped at you. You didn't deserve that.`
- `I was embarrassed, and I took it out on you.`
- `I kept saying I was joking, but I could tell it hurt.`
- `I should have told the truth earlier.`
- `I'm sorry for what I did. I want to fix what I can.`

Avoid apology forms like:
- `I'm sorry you felt that way`
- `I had no choice`
- `I'm the worst person ever`

## Pick-a-Path Branch Logic

If this theme is used in interactive story structure, choices should map to emotionally meaningful paths, not good-kid vs bad-kid binaries.

### Core branch model

Branch point 1: immediate response
- lash out
- shut down / avoid
- pause and observe

Branch point 2: social strategy
- defend self publicly
- pull one person aside
- ask for help
- leave and regroup

Branch point 3: repair or boundary
- apologize
- explain impact
- set a boundary
- double down

Branch point 4: resolution style
- reconnect
- coexist with distance
- find a new ally
- accept incomplete repair

### Consequence logic

`lash out`
- short-term relief
- increases social fallout
- creates repair burden later

`shut down / avoid`
- lowers immediate conflict
- increases misunderstanding or isolation
- may preserve dignity in the moment but costs clarity

`pause and observe`
- hardest choice upfront
- usually gives best access to honest information
- unlocks stronger boundary or repair options

`defend self publicly`
- can protect status
- risks escalation if done while flooded

`pull one person aside`
- lowers performance pressure
- improves odds of real understanding

`ask for help`
- should read as strategic, not helpless
- best used when power is uneven or rumor is spreading

`double down`
- should create believable emotional cost
- not villain punishment, but distance, mistrust, or missed opportunity

### Success criteria for branches

Not every ending needs friendship restored.

Winning outcomes include:
- saying the true thing without exploding
- leaving safely instead of retaliating
- apologizing specifically
- learning the conflict is not fully fixable
- finding one steady ally
- protecting dignity while staying honest

## Sample Theme Openings

These are opening patterns, not final story copy.

### Anger

Opening frame:
- Something unfair has just happened.
- The protagonist feels heat first, thoughts second.

Example opening:
- By the time the teacher said, "Let's move on," the moment had already welded itself to the inside of Jordan's ribs. Max had interrupted twice, taken Jordan's idea, and somehow Jordan was the one being told to settle down. Every sound in the room got sharper. Even the scrape of chairs felt insulting.

### Anxiety

Opening frame:
- The protagonist is anticipating exposure or failure.
- Their brain is running ahead of the scene.

Example opening:
- Eli had not gone up to present yet, but his body had apparently decided it was already happening. His notes were steady in his hand; the problem was the hand. It wouldn't stop shaking. Every time someone near the front laughed, his mind grabbed the sound and used it against him.

### Embarrassment

Opening frame:
- A social mistake has happened in public.
- The protagonist's first impulse is to disappear or rewrite the moment.

Example opening:
- The joke landed wrong. Not "nobody laughed" wrong. Worse. A few people did laugh, and then Priya saw Sam's face and understood exactly one second too late what she had turned into entertainment.

### Loneliness

Opening frame:
- The protagonist is physically near others but emotionally outside the group.
- The scene should feel specific, not abstract.

Example opening:
- There was still space at the lunch table. That was the strange part. No one had told Mara to leave. No one had moved her tray. But three conversations were happening around her like zipped doors, and by the time she found a place to look, everyone else already belonged to the moment more than she did.

## Tone Shift: Cute to More Mature

The age 9-12 version should shift from whimsical reassurance to grounded emotional drama.

### Language

Use:
- restrained emotional language
- specific social stakes
- short, sharp dialogue
- dry humor where appropriate

Avoid:
- exclamation-heavy copy
- animal-metaphor feelings talk
- babyish reassurance
- overt praise language like `good job using your calm-down tools`

### Story framing

Use:
- pressure
- misunderstandings
- fallout
- second chances
- believable dignity

Avoid:
- magic fixes
- instant group hugs
- adults explaining the moral
- a companion acting like a therapist

### Visual direction

For ages 9-12:
- more atmosphere, less mascot energy
- symbolic backgrounds over cartoon emotion characters
- mood-board, graphic-novel, or cinematic cues
- facial expressions should be subtle enough to feel real

For teens:
- editorial and understated
- abstract or symbolic emotion visuals
- no cute emotion creatures

### Humor

Allowed:
- awkward humor
- sharp observational humor
- one-line relief after tension

Not allowed:
- slapstick that trivializes the feeling
- goofy voices in serious scenes

## Story Engine Requirements

Any older-kid `Big Feelings` story should include:
- one clearly legible trigger
- one impulsive or avoidant first move
- one consequence that changes the social field
- one regulation beat that restores choice
- one direct line of truth, boundary, or apology
- one ending that improves clarity, safety, or connection

Optional but strong:
- mixed-feeling naming
- partial repair instead of full repair
- a friend who is imperfect but reachable

## Guardrails

Do not write:
- lecture paragraphs
- therapist-sounding dialogue from peers
- shame-heavy punishment arcs
- stories where calming means emotional suppression
- endings that require forgiveness from the hurt party

Do write:
- emotional intensity with dignity
- mistakes with believable fallout
- repair with uncertainty
- support that feels respectful

## Acceptance Criteria

The ages 9-12 version is successful if:
- it sounds socially credible to a 10-12 year old
- children can see themselves in the trigger and fallout
- the story names feelings more precisely than just mad/sad/scared
- calming changes the next choice rather than ending the emotion
- repair is modeled as brave, specific, and optional for the other person to accept
- the overall experience feels safe and supportive without ever sounding instructional
