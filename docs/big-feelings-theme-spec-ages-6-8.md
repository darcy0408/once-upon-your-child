# Big Feelings Theme Spec for Ages 6-8

## Purpose
Design a "Big Feelings" story theme for ages 6-8 that helps children:
- name feelings with more nuance
- calm without repressing the feeling
- see clear cause and effect
- practice repair after mistakes

This is a story theme inside the existing app, not a separate mode.

## Experience Principles
- Playful, not preachy: emotional growth happens through story action, not direct instruction.
- Slightly more nuanced: children can hold two feelings at once and notice intensity.
- Safe cause/effect: choices lead to understandable outcomes without shame spirals.
- Calm plus repair: the goal is not "stop feeling," it is "feel, steady, then choose what to do next."
- Agency first: the child picks what the hero does, and the story shows what changed.

## Target Age Calibration
- Voice: warm, playful, storybook-style with personality
- Reading level: easy to moderate words, 6-8 friendly
- Choice complexity: mild strategy, never abstract therapy language
- Emotional complexity:
  - one main feeling plus an optional second feeling
  - simple trigger/context
  - one calming tool
  - one repair action if needed

## Theme Positioning
Use "Big Feelings" as one of the five story themes.

Theme promise:
"A story where big feelings show up fast, the world reacts, and your hero figures out what helps next."

Theme flavor:
- expressive weather, color, creature, or object metaphors
- feelings have motion: fizzing, stomping, wobbling, buzzing, shrinking, softening
- consequences are visible and immediate
- repair is framed as brave action, not punishment

## Core Loop
1. Name what the hero feels.
2. Notice what happened.
3. Pick what the hero does first.
4. See the effect.
5. If things go wrong, try calming or repair.
6. Continue the adventure with the feeling changed, not erased.

## Screen Flow

### 1. Theme Select
User selects `Big Feelings` from the standard theme list.

Theme card copy:
"Stormy thoughts, stomp-y feet, wiggly worries, and brave do-overs."

### 2. Feeling Check-In
Goal: name the main feeling with slightly richer vocabulary than the youngest flow.

Prompt:
"What feeling is biggest right now?"

UI:
- 6 big starter feeling tiles
- tap one to open 4-5 child-friendly "more like..." options
- optional "also a little..." second feeling row
- simple intensity picker: `small`, `medium`, `huge`

Starter feelings:
- angry
- worried
- sad
- frustrated
- embarrassed
- excited

### 3. Trigger + Context
Goal: anchor the story in a recognizable moment.

Prompt:
"What kicked the feeling awake?"

UI:
- first choose a trigger
- then choose where/who was around

### 4. Story Setup Card
Shows the hero state before the story begins.

Format:
"[Name] feels [main feeling], maybe a bit [second feeling], after [trigger] at/in [context]."

Example:
"Milo feels frustrated, maybe a bit embarrassed, after making a mistake during art class."

CTA:
`Start the story`

### 5. Story Opening
The opening scene mirrors the chosen feeling and trigger through playful story logic.

Requirements:
- show body cue
- show feeling word naturally
- show the first consequence of the feeling
- end on a choice point

### 6. First Choice: What Does the Hero Do First?
This is the first pick-a-path moment.

Choice types:
- react fast
- pause and steady
- get help or use words

The "react fast" option is allowed. It should not be the "bad kid" path. It creates more mess, then opens repair.

### 7. Coping Tool Moment
After the first outcome, offer 2-3 tools matched to the feeling pattern.

Prompt style:
"What might help the feeling wobble less?"

Important:
- tools are for making space, not making the feeling disappear
- tool effects are concrete and short

### 8. Second Choice: What Does the Hero Do Next?
Now the hero acts with slightly more steadiness.

Choice types:
- solve the practical problem
- say what happened
- repair with someone affected

### 9. Reflection Beat
A brief, non-teachy pause inside the story.

Examples:
- "Huh. The storm in her chest was still there, but now it had stopped throwing chairs."
- "He was still mad. He was just mad in a way that could think."

### 10. Ending Type
Each branch ends in one of these outcome shapes:
- calm and continue
- repair and reconnect
- ask for help and try again
- partial win with honest do-over

No ending should imply the child must become cheerful.

### 11. End Reflection
Short closing prompt, framed as noticing, not teaching.

Examples:
- "What changed first: the problem, the feeling, or the choice?"
- "Which part felt bravest?"
- "What helped the hero make a better next move?"

## Coping Tool Selection Model
Choose tools based on what the feeling seems to need.

### Model Goals
- lower intensity enough for choice
- keep dignity intact
- match the tool to the moment
- create visible effect in story action

### Decision Rule

#### If the feeling is high-energy and outward
Examples:
- angry
- furious
- irritated
- ready-to-pop

Needs:
- body release
- distance from the spark
- words after the body settles

Offer tools like:
- dragon breaths
- stomp-and-stop
- squeeze hands, then release
- count five things the hero can see

#### If the feeling is high-energy and inward
Examples:
- worried
- nervous
- jumpy
- what-if-y

Needs:
- safety cue
- predictability
- anchoring

Offer tools like:
- hand-on-heart and slow breath
- spot three safe things
- ask one true question
- hold a pocket object or story charm

#### If the feeling is blocked / stuck
Examples:
- frustrated
- overwhelmed
- mixed up
- trying-so-hard

Needs:
- break the task down
- reset pressure
- get help or switch strategy

Offer tools like:
- tiny-step plan
- shake out the "stuck sparks"
- ask for one clue
- take a restart minute

### Tool Selection Rules
- always show 1 body tool
- always show 1 thinking or noticing tool
- show 1 social/help-seeking tool when another person matters
- never present more than 3 tools at once

### Tool Effect Pattern
Each tool should visibly change one story variable:
- body gets steadier
- thoughts get clearer
- voice gets kinder
- hero notices a new option

## Pick-a-Path Choice Design

### Choice Structure
Every major choice should include:
- 1 impulsive but understandable option
- 1 calming or grounding option
- 1 connection, problem-solving, or repair option

### Writing Rules
- choices should sound active and playful
- verbs first
- no therapy jargon
- no obvious "good kid" answer

### Example Choice Frame
Instead of:
- Calm down
- Use coping strategy

Use:
- stomp to the thunder drum bridge
- hide behind the moon-crate and take three dragon breaths
- tell Juniper what went wrong before the fizz-whisk machine explodes again

### Outcome Rules
- every choice changes the scene
- impulsive choices can escalate the mess
- calming choices create thinking space
- connection choices may solve faster, but can feel harder emotionally
- repair choices should feel brave, not scolded

## Example Branches

### Branch A: Angry After a Fairness Problem
Setup:
- main feeling: angry
- trigger: someone cut in line
- context: playground with peers

Opening beat:
- the hero’s face gets hot
- they march forward
- the rope bridge to the slide tower starts snapping sparks

Choice 1:
- shove past to get your spot back
- stomp to the side and blow three dragon breaths
- say, "Hey, I was waiting," to the line-cutter

Outcomes:
- `shove past`
  - the line gets messier
  - another kid gets upset
  - hero feels powerful for one second, then extra hot inside
  - next step becomes repair plus calm
- `dragon breaths`
  - sparks shrink enough to think
  - hero still feels mad
  - now they can use words clearly
- `say it`
  - the other kid may listen, deny, or look surprised
  - social tension stays interesting

Repair path:
- hero says what happened
- checks if someone got bumped
- asks for a do-over in line

Ending:
- not "and then no one was mad"
- instead: "The mad feeling got smaller when the hero got their feet and words working together."

### Branch B: Worried Before Something New
Setup:
- main feeling: worried
- trigger: I had to try something new
- context: classroom with teacher

Opening beat:
- the chalkboard map swirls
- the hero’s tummy does flip-flops
- the new task feels like a hallway with too many doors

Choice 1:
- blurt, "I can’t do this!"
- touch the lucky compass charm and name three safe things
- ask the teacher what the first step is

Outcomes:
- `blurt`
  - class attention swings toward hero
  - embarrassment joins the worry
  - next step needs calming plus repair of the moment
- `name three safe things`
  - breathing slows
  - task still looks new, but not giant
  - hero can choose a first step
- `ask first step`
  - adult support creates clarity
  - shows help-seeking as strong, not babyish

Repair path:
- if hero snapped or ran, they return and say, "I got worry-jumbled. Can I try again?"

Ending:
- the new thing is still new
- the hero learns the first step can be small

### Branch C: Frustrated After a Mistake
Setup:
- main feeling: frustrated
- trigger: my plan did not work
- context: art table with a friend nearby

Opening beat:
- the paint-mixing machine burps glitter mud
- the hero’s shoulders bunch up
- their masterpiece looks wronger every second

Choice 1:
- crumple the paper and groan
- push the chair back and shake out the stuck sparks
- tell your friend, "This is not going how I hoped"

Outcomes:
- `crumple`
  - accidental mess spreads
  - friend may get splashed
  - repair becomes relevant
- `shake out the stuck sparks`
  - body reset first
  - hero notices one part that can still be saved
- `tell your friend`
  - connection lowers pressure
  - friend may offer a clue or encouraging idea

Repair path:
- clean up spilled paint
- apologize if someone was affected
- try a smaller next step

Ending:
- the picture may not match the original plan
- the hero still makes something they can stand by

## Sample Story Openings
These are tone references, not final scripts.

### Angry Opening
Nico knew the feeling before he had a name for it. It was the hot, stompy kind that made his sneakers want to argue with the ground. One second he was waiting his turn at the comet slide, and the next second Ezra zipped right in front of him like the line was invisible. The clouds above the playground puffed red around the edges. Even the slide gave a tiny angry squeak. Nico’s hands curled into fists. He could charge forward, yank the words right out of his chest, or do something harder: stop long enough to choose them.

### Worried Opening
Lena stared at the classroom door with her hand on the star-shaped knob. Today was Puppet Share Day, which had seemed like an excellent idea yesterday when nobody was looking at her. Now her tummy felt full of hopping frogs. Inside the room, cheerful puppet voices squeaked and boinged. In Lena’s mind, every possible terrible thing lined up neatly: drop the puppet, forget the voice, trip on the rug, turn the color of ketchup. Her worry was not quiet. It was wearing tap shoes. She took one tiny breath and wondered what would help first.

### Frustrated Opening
Jae had a plan. It was a very good plan. A magnificent plan. A plan involving cardboard wings, silver paint, and exactly six gold stars on the tail of the rocket. Then the glue blobbed, the wing drooped, and one of the stars stuck to Jae’s elbow instead of the ship. "Oh, come on," he groaned, as the art table gave a sad little creak like it agreed. His chest tightened. His eyebrows squeezed together. The rocket looked less like "ready for the moon" and more like "fell off the moon on the way down." Jae could smash ahead, step back, or let someone into the disaster.

## Reflection Moments That Do Not Feel Teachy
These are woven into narration, not presented as adult advice.

### Mid-Story Reflection Beats
- "Mira noticed she was still mad. She just was not mad in a running-with-scissors kind of way anymore."
- "The worry did not leave. It just scooted over enough for one useful thought."
- "Owen’s mistake was still a mistake. Now it looked more fixable than giant."
- "Her cheeks still felt warm, but her words had stopped tripping over each other."

### End Reflection Prompts
- "What changed after the hero slowed down a little?"
- "Which choice made the biggest mess? Which one opened the story back up?"
- "When did the hero get ready to repair?"
- "What would your hero try first next time?"

## Repair Design
Repair should appear when:
- the hero hurts someone’s feelings
- the hero makes a bigger mess while upset
- the hero says something sharp
- the hero runs away from a problem and comes back

### Repair Moves
- say what happened honestly
- check if someone is okay
- offer a fix or cleanup
- ask for a do-over
- try again with different words

### Repair Writing Rule
Repair is strongest when it costs a little courage.

Example:
Instead of "sorry" ending the problem instantly, the story shows:
- the hero walks back
- admits what happened
- helps fix the mess
- reconnects through action

## Success Criteria
The theme is working if the story consistently helps children:
- choose a more precise feeling word than just "mad" or "sad"
- see that feelings can stay while behavior changes
- understand how a first reaction affects what happens next
- experience calming as preparation for action, not emotional shutdown
- experience repair as brave and normal after mistakes

## Content Guardrails
- do not moralize
- do not use therapist or classroom lesson language
- do not force cheerful endings
- do not shame impulsive behavior
- do not present calming as "make the feeling go away"
- do keep the world magical, responsive, and playful
- do let the hero recover dignity after mistakes
